01 · THE CLASSICAL WORLD

In the classical world, every answer is definite

Flip a coin and look. It is heads, or it is tails — one or the other, never both at once.

Look again tomorrow: the answer stands. Measuring simply reports a fact that was already there.

Two possible answers. Nothing in between. This is the world of the bit.

H
heads · 1
or
T
tails · 0
02 · PROBABILITY

At small scales, answers become probabilities

Zoom in far enough and certainty crumbles. Before you look, the honest statement is a bet:

heads with probability p, tails with the remaining 1 − p.

Every possible belief about the coin becomes a single point on the interval [0, 1].

03 · TWO KINDS OF NOT-KNOWING

One number hides where the uncertainty comes from

THE FAIR COIN

Genuinely random. Every flip is fresh chance — flip it forever and the 50/50 never budges.

Statistical uncertainty — it is there to stay.

THE MYSTERY COIN

Already decided — it always lands the same side. You just don't know which. One peek and you know everything.

Systematic uncertainty — you can learn it away.

Before the first flip, both coins are 50/50. On the interval they are the same point — one number cannot tell wisdom-to-come from permanent chance.

Spoiler: a quantum coin is neither — nothing is written down before you look. Hold that thought for the bonus.

04 · THE BERNOULLI CIRCLE

A second number separates the two coins

Give every coin two scores: its average outcome, and its spread — how much its flips wobble.

The fair coin wobbles maximally. The mystery coin, averaged over both its possibilities, doesn't wobble at all.

Plot average across, spread up: every definite coin lands on one arc — the upper half of the Bernoulli circle.

spread = √(p(1−p)) → a circle of radius ½
05 · MIXED BELIEFS

Mixed beliefs fill the half-disk

Not sure which coin is in your hand? Your belief is a blend — and it slides along the straight line between the candidates.

Blends reach every point inside the arc, and none can escape it.

On the rim: coins you know exactly — chance that stays.

Deeper inside: doubt about the coin itself — flips can teach it away. Dead center: total ignorance.

06 · THE HIDDEN TWIN

Every point has a hidden twin

A half-circle is suspicious — half of what?

Mirror any point P below the line: the twin P′ gives exactly the same odds. No coin flip will ever tell them apart.

But the triangle T–state–H circles one way above the line and the other way below. Same lengths, opposite orientation.

Keep the orientation and the twins become distinct: the full Bernoulli circle.

07 · THE BLOCH BALL

The switch becomes a dial, the circle becomes a ball

The twin was a two-position switch: above or below the line.

Quantum replaces the switch with a continuous dial φ. At every set of odds, a whole wheel of twins now stands upright on the T–H axle.

The circle inflates into a ball — the Bloch ball. Definite states on the glassy surface, mixed beliefs inside, total ignorance at the center.

the sign ± grows into an angle φ ∈ [0°, 360°)
08 · WHY IT MATTERS

A richer data structure is the power of quantum

bit — two answers
→
probability — an interval
→
a sign — the full circle
→
a dial — the quantum ball

A qubit still answers with a single bit. But between questions it carries, blends, and steers information in this whole ball of states — and that is the room quantum computers work in.

And richer data comes with richer handles: a bit you can only swap — a qubit you can rotate, any angle, around any axis. More on that in the bonus.

BONUS

What makes it really quantum

measuring, in three moves: ask · rotate · compare

BONUS 1 · MEASURING

Measuring is asking "which end?"

You may never ask a state "where are you?". You may only pick a diameter and ask: which end?

The odds are drawn in the picture: the chance of an answer is the squared distance to the opposite end. Far from "always T" means: probably answers H.

Every state is 100% certain about exactly one question — the diameter through it. Randomness is a mismatch between the state and the question you chose.

And unlike the mystery coin, the answer is not sitting there waiting: it comes into being at the moment you ask.

BONUS 2 · INTERFERENCE

Rotating the question is real physics

The plain flip is the horizontal diameter — and its two ends sit on the mirror line itself. That is exactly why it never sees the twins.

Any tilted question breaks the symmetry: P leans toward one end, its twin P′ toward the other. The hidden sign becomes visible.

And tilting is not fantasy: rotate the whole system first, then flip. That is all an interferometer or a polarizer does.

One more rule: after answering, the state becomes its answer — repeat the question, repeat the answer.

BONUS 3 · OPERATIONS

A bit swaps; a qubit rotates

A bit has exactly one non-trivial move: swap heads and tails. That is the whole toolbox.

On the ball, every diameter is an axis: rotate by any angle around any of them — full flips along tilted diameters, quarter-turns, the gentlest nudge.

These continuous rotations are the gates of a quantum computer — and the previous slide already showed the hardware: an interferometer rotates before it flips.

So the power of quantum is twofold: more information in the state, and a continuous range of ways to steer it.

BONUS 4 · DISTANCE

Measuring is judging how far apart states sit

In the end, every measurement compares: how far is your state from the answer-points of your question?

Distances are not read on a flat ruler. Bend the disk into a bowl — the Bernoulli hemisphere — and walk along its surface.

The fair coin and the mystery coin, forever identical in their odds, sit a crisp π/4 apart on the bowl.

P(pass) = cos²(distance)cos²(π/4) = ½

So the mystery coin passes the "are you the fair coin?" test exactly half the time. Geometry, cashed in as probability.
