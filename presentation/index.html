PROLOGUE · 1 — WHY QUANTUM, WHY NOW

Computing grew because chips kept shrinking

For five decades computing improved for one dominant reason: transistors kept getting smaller, so ever more of them fit on a chip.

On a log scale the count climbs as a straight line — a doubling every couple of years, from thousands in 1970 toward a trillion per package around 2030.

The whole digital world rides this one curve.

PROLOGUE · 2 — THE END OF SMALL

Shrinking has reached the atoms themselves

Follow the shrinking to its end: 90 nm, 65, 45, 32, 22, 14, 10, 7, 3… the industry now speaks of the angstrom era — a tenth of a nanometer. The width of a single atom.

A transistor's gate is a few dozen atoms across.

And down here the comfortable rules — definite places, definite answers — stop applying. The shrinking that powered everything has delivered us to a different rulebook.

PROLOGUE · 3 — YOUNG'S DOUBLE SLIT

Two slits sort particles from waves

Tennis balls thrown at a wall with two slits: some bounce, some pass. Mark the hits on a second wall — two strips, the shape of the slits. That is what particles do.

Light of one color at the same wall: each slit births a fresh ripple. Peak meets peak — bright; peak meets trough — dark. The second wall shows a stripy ladder. That is what waves do.

Two slits, two signatures. Remember them both.

PROLOGUE · 4 — THE NEW RULEBOOK

Electrons build the ladder — until you watch

Now fire electrons — solid particles, tiny tennis balls. You'd bet on two strips. But the screen builds the ladder, dot by dot — each electron lands as one definite dot, yet the dots pile up in a wave's pattern. Even one at a time.

Weirder: place a detector at the slits to see which slit each electron takes — and the ladder collapses into the two strips. Watching changes the answer.

Definite paths are gone; what remains are probabilities of the next dot. So take the statistics seriously — just statistics, at first.

PROLOGUE · 5 — THE ROAD AHEAD

Not statistics plus magic — statistics with the details kept

The usual story: quantum is not statistics but runs on mysterious new numbers, the amplitudes.

Our narrative: quantum is about those amplitudes — but they are just statistics without the details thrown away. Track where the uncertainty came from, and whether it can be learned away.

Arch I simply refuses to forget. Keep the details, and the space of states grows by itself.

Nothing quantum will be added along the way — only detail kept. Watch the structure grow.

ARCH I

Not all uncertainty is alike

starts statistical · ends at the qubit, ball and all

I · 1 — FROM CERTAINTY TO CHANCE

Definite answers give way to bets

In the classical world every answer is definite: heads or tails, one or the other, never both. Measuring reports a fact that was already there.

Zoom in far enough and certainty crumbles. Before you look, the honest statement is a bet: heads with probability p, tails with the rest.

Every belief about the coin becomes one point on the interval [0, 1].

I · 2 — TWO KINDS OF NOT-KNOWING

One number hides where the uncertainty comes from

THE FAIR COIN

Genuinely random. Every flip is fresh chance — flip it forever and the 50/50 never budges.

Statistical uncertainty — it is there to stay.

THE MYSTERY COIN

Always lands the same side — you just don't know which. One peek and you know everything.

Systematic uncertainty — you can learn it away.

Before the first flip, both coins are 50/50. On the interval they are the same point — one number cannot tell wisdom-to-come from permanent chance.

I · 3 — THE BERNOULLI CIRCLE

A second number opens the half-disk

Give every coin two scores: its average outcome, and its spread — how much its flips wobble.

Coins you know exactly land on one arc — the upper half of the Bernoulli circle. The fair coin at the top; the averaged mystery coin at the center, finally separated.

Not sure which coin you hold? Blends slide along straight lines and fill the whole half-disk: rim — chance that stays; interior — doubt you can learn away.

spread = √(p(1−p)) → a circle of radius ½
I · 4 — TWO SCHOOL THEOREMS

Thales and Pythagoras hide inside the coin

Pick any belief on the arc and connect it to the two corners. Thales: a diameter is always seen at a right angle — the two chords are perpendicular.

Their lengths are exactly √p and √(1−p). Pythagoras finishes it: legs squared add to the hypotenuse squared — and T–H has length 1, so p + (1−p) = 1.

The coin's arithmetic is literally a right triangle.

Use the two lengths as coordinates of their own: (√p, √(1−p)) lives on the unit circle — the amplitudes, at some angle α.

I · 5 — THE SQUARE-ROOT EMBEDDING

One turn of the dial, two laps of the coin

The lift is a map: send every coin p to its amplitude pair (√p, √(1−p)) on the unit circle — the square-root embedding.

Now sweep the angle α once around the unit circle and watch the coin: its Bernoulli point runs around twice — the Bernoulli angle is 2α.

amplitude angle α → Bernoulli angle 2α

The first quarter-turn already covers every coin once. Every belief owns two amplitude points, α and −α — the twin is already waiting.

I · 6 — THE HIDDEN TWIN

Orientation completes the circle — the real part of a qubit

Mirror any point P below the line: the twin P′ gives exactly the same odds. No coin flip will ever tell them apart.

But the triangle T–state–H circles one way above the line and the other way below. Same lengths, opposite orientation.

Keep the orientation and the twins become distinct: the full circle, and with blends the full disk.

This disk is the real slice of a qubit — one dial short of the full thing.

I · 7 — THE COMPLEX DIAL

The switch becomes a dial, the disk becomes a ball

The twin was a two-position switch: above or below the line.

Nature upgrades it to a continuous dial φ: at every set of odds, a whole wheel of twins stands upright on the T–H axle.

The disk inflates into the Bloch ball — the complete state space of a qubit. Definite states on the surface, mixed beliefs inside, total ignorance at the center.

the sign ± grows into an angle φ ∈ [0°, 360°)
I · 8 — ROTATIONS

A bit swaps; a qubit rotates

A bit has exactly one non-trivial move: swap heads and tails. That is the whole toolbox.

On the ball, every diameter is an axis: rotate by any angle around any of them. Rotating tilts the question — and tilted axles finally see the twins the plain flip is blind to.

This is real hardware, not fantasy: rotate the whole system, then flip — that is all an interferometer or a polarizer does.

These continuous rotations are the gates of a quantum computer: more information in the state, and more ways to steer it.

I · 9 — WHY IT MATTERS

A richer data structure — living in the wild

bit — two answers
→
probability — an interval
→
a sign — the full circle
→
a dial — the quantum ball

More information in the state. More ways to steer it.

But the real power: these qubits live in the wild.

Electrons, photons, atoms are qubits — nature provides them; we only need to tame them.

ARCH II

How far apart are two coins?

starts statistical · ends at the ruler of quantum information

II · 1 — A NATURAL ANSWER, TESTED

The flat ruler lies about distance

Obvious answer: the gap on the interval. Let's test it with two duels, each a gap of exactly 0.10.

Duel A — a 0.50-coin vs a 0.60-coin: you need roughly 140 flips to name the coin with confidence.

Duel B — a 0.00-coin vs a 0.10-coin: roughly 20 flips — a single head settles it.

Same gap, tenfold difference in real cost. Distance should measure how hard it is to tell apart — and the flat ruler gets it wrong.

II · 2 — THE HONEST RULER

Lift to the circle, walk the arc

Arch I built the fix already: lift each coin to its point on the Bernoulli circle, and measure along the arc.

Near the edges the arc stretches — exactly where a few flips say the most. Duel B's short flat gap becomes a long arc.

This arc has a classical name: the Bhattacharyya angle between two distributions.

And it is the ruler that counts: the flips you need grow as 1/(arc)². Equal arcs cost equal flips — anywhere on the circle.

overlap of two coins = cos(arc between them)
II · 3 — INTO THE DISK

Bend every diameter: the disk becomes a bowl

One distance is still missing — the one we started with: fair coin vs mystery coin, inside the disk.

Every diameter of the disk is a fresh copy of the interval [0, 1]. And we just learned what to do with an interval: bend it into its Bernoulli semicircle.

Do it to every diameter at once. The bent arcs agree wherever they cross and share one summit — together they form the Bernoulli hemisphere.

The mystery coin, at the center of every diameter, rides up to the summit.

II · 4 — THE RULER OF QUANTUM INFORMATION

The walk on the bowl is the Bures distance

On the bowl, distance means the geodesic: the shortest walk along the surface.

Fair coin on the rim, mystery coin at the summit: a crisp π/4 apart — forever identical in their odds, cleanly separated by the walk.

Its official name: the Bures distance — the very distance quantum information theory uses between quantum states (the density matrices hiding in your disk).

A purely statistical question, answered honestly, has handed us quantum's own ruler. Arch II closes.

ARCH III

What does the distance mean?

the keystone — where arch I's states meet arch II's ruler

III · 1 — MEASURING, THE QUANTUM WAY

Never ask where — pick an axle, ask which end

Rule of the game: you may never ask a state "where are you?". You pick a diameter — an axle through the disk — and ask: which end?

The odds are drawn in the picture: the chance of an answer is the squared distance to the opposite end.

Every state is 100% sure about exactly one axle — its own diameter. Randomness is a mismatch between state and question.

And unlike the mystery coin, the answer is not sitting there waiting — it comes into being at the moment you ask.

III · 2 — THE ARCHES MEET

Distance cashes in as probability

Build a tester for "are you the fair coin?" — simply the axle through the fair coin and the anti-coin.

A candidate passes with probability cos² of its Bures distance to the fair coin.

Check it: the mystery coin sits π/4 away — cos²(π/4) = ½ — it passes half the time. Geometry, cashed in as probability.

P(pass) = cos²(distance)distance = arccos(√P(pass))

Arch II's ruler predicts arch III's odds: measuring is judging how far apart states sit.

III · 3 — THE KEYSTONE

Two honest questions built the quantum stage

ARCH I · what is a state? → the disk
ARCH II · how far apart? → the bowl
ARCH III · what does it mean? → the odds

Neither question contained any quantum physics. Answering them honestly forced this whole stage into existence — and quantum mechanics is the discovery that nature performs on it.

You built a qubit from a coin — nature had built it first.

BONUS

The evidence room

a nice story, or an actual construction? — the proof, room by room

BONUS 1 · COUNTING THE FLIPS

Race the two duels — with an honest referee

Arch II promised that the circle predicts how many flips you need. Time to collect.

The referee is Wald's sequential test: every flip adds its evidence to a running total, and the moment the total crosses a wall — set for 5% error — the verdict is called.

Duel A wanders between the walls for ≈ 140 flips.

Duel B plods through tails — until one head ends the game on the spot. ≈ 20 flips.

BONUS 2 · BY HAND

Both flip counts, on the back of an envelope

DUEL A · 0.50 VS 0.60

After n flips the head-count wobbles by about 0.5/√n. The verdict is safe once the wobble shrinks below the half-gap, 0.05.

That takes ≈ 270 flips at fixed length — the thrifty referee averages ≈ 145. Same order.

DUEL B · 0.00 VS 0.10

The 0.00-coin cannot produce a head — a single H ends the game. The only mistake left: the 0.10-coin sulking through all tails, chance 0.9ⁿ.

Demand 0.9ⁿ ≤ 5% and you get ≈ 29 flips, worst case — and one head usually ends it much sooner.

Same gap of 0.10 — a factor of ten in flips. The envelope agrees with the race.

BONUS 3 · ONE LAW BEHIND BOTH

The overlap of two coins is the cosine of their arc

For the best possible test, the error shrinks like the n-th power of the overlap between the two coins.

Lift each coin to the circle, and the overlap turns out to be nothing but the cosine of the arc between them. Flip budget: 1/(arc)².

overlap = cos(arc)flips ≈ 2·ln(1/ε) / (arc)²

Check: duel A spans an arc of 0.101, duel B spans 0.322 — the circle predicts ×10, the race clocked ×10, the flat ruler said ×1.

BONUS 4 · WHY THE ARC, OF ALL RULERS

Not just a good ruler — the only honest one

LOCAL

One flip says most near the edges — the flat ruler's blind spot. Demand a scale where every flip buys equal progress, and you are forced onto the circle.

GLOBAL

Over any separation the cost of telling coins apart is set by cos(arc): equal arcs cost equal flips, wherever they sit.

UNIQUE

Čencov's theorem: an honest distance may only shrink when you post-process data — and the arc is the only ruler with that property.

Keep the cosine in your pocket: it returns as the quantum overlap —

the Bures walk of arch II, and the cos²(π/4) = ½ of arch III.

BONUS 5 · UNDER THE HOOD

Your disk was a density matrix all along

Every point of the disk is secretly a small 2 × 2 table — physicists call it the density matrix. Its top row holds exactly your two coordinates: the odds and the orientation.

Mixing beliefs = averaging tables. That is why blends were straight chords all along.

And arch II's walk on the bowl is precisely the textbook Bures distance between density matrices — the tutorial has been doing quantum information's bookkeeping in a coin costume.

BONUS 6 · THE STATE'S OWN AXES

Every state chooses its own diameter

The mixer left an ambiguity: many chords pass through one interior point — many recipes, one belief. The matrix breaks the tie: every state carries one distinguished chord, its own diameter.

That is the whole quantum dictionary: the diameter's endpoints are the eigen-states — the only ones that answer with certainty; the eigenvalues are the values on the ends, or the weights λ± over them.

Sound familiar? Data science does this daily as PCA: let the object choose its own axes. Spectral decomposition is PCA applied to a belief. Eigen-something = a diameter with two endpoints. Nothing more.

BONUS ⊗ BONUS

The summit needs a partner

not two bonuses side by side — their tensor product: entanglement

⊗ 1 — ONLY ONE DIRECTION LEFT

No room inside the qubit — go sideways

The tutorial's great trade: uncertainty about a coin can be rewritten as uncertainty inside a state. The lift did it for the fair coin.

Can the summit — the mystery coin, the biggest lift of all — make the same trade? Not inside the qubit: the sign is spent, the dial is spent.

One direction remains: sideways. Give the coin a partner.

You already own one: every coin is a pair of faces, glued in disagreement by the mint — 2 × 2 = 4 options, cut down to 2. Perfect correlation is cheap.

⊗ 2 — TWO FACTORIES, ONE TESTER

Certain as a pair, uncertain in each half

THE GLUED FACTORY

Welds two whole coins in agreement: each pair is secretly HH or secretly TT, 50/50. The choice is a fact — someone at the factory could jot it in a notebook and ship it along. There is something to peek at.

THE BELL FACTORY

Ships the Bell pair (HH + TT)/√2 — a superposition, amplitudes added before squaring. No fact was ever created: no notebook — not even a blank one.

Ask both halves the same rotated question. Each half alone stays a perfect mystery coin — but the agreement tells the factories apart. The Bell pair answers every joint question with certainty.

⊗ 3 — A DISK YOU HAVE SEEN BEFORE

The Bell pair is the fair coin of a joint disk

The whole contest happened in the span of HH and TT — the agreement corner. Two dimensions: by our own construction, a Bernoulli disk. Same picture, new labels.

The glued mixture N is the corner's mystery coin, at the center. The Bell pair Φ is its fair coin, at the top of the rim — π/4 apart, the old number one floor up.

distance(N, Φ) = arccos √½ = π/4

One hand on one coin can only work the corner's plain flip — blind to orientation. The revealing questions are joint: both coins in one apparatus turn the dial no single hand can reach.

⊗ 4 — BUILD THE PARTNER

Purify any state in three familiar moves

1 · READ

Read the state spectrally: weight λ₊ on eigen-coin e₊, weight λ₋ on e₋. It is a state that was prepared, then forgotten.

2 · SHIP

Make it physical: the glued factory ships e₊e₊ or e₋e₋ per branch. Cover the partner: the original returns — notebook included.

3 · LIFT

Superpose the same shipments — chord to arc, weights to square roots. A rim point, no notebook: purification.

N = λ₊ · (e₊e₊) + λ₋ · (e₋e₋)a mixture — the + means "or": either/or, decided
Ψ = √λ₊ · (e₊e₊) + √λ₋ · (e₋e₋)a superposition — the + means "and": both at once

One-sided questions cannot tell Ψ from the notebook.

Only questions that touch both halves at once see the difference.

⊗ 5 — WHAT THE LIFT HEIGHT REALLY WAS

The lift height is the entanglement

In the eigen-corner's disk the notebook N sits on the floor; Ψ stands directly above it on the rim. The climb between them is the lift — one floor up.

C = 2·√(λ₊λ₋) = 2 × lift height

The concurrence — the standard measure of entanglement — is exactly twice the lift height. The vertical coordinate you have drawn since the bowl was built is the entanglement with the purifying partner.

At the rim: C = 0 — pure states purify themselves. At the center: C = 1 — the mystery coin, honestly purified, is the Bell pair.

⊗ 6 — THE CLEVEREST FACTORY

Bell's test: no answer sheet passes the wall

A last classical hope: ship every pair a complete answer sheet — one pre-agreed answer per question, identical in both halves. It fools the same-question tester perfectly.

So ask the halves different questions — the CHSH game. The algebra is merciless: every sheet wins at most 75% of rounds. That wall is Bell's inequality.

The Bell pair, answering by the cosine ruler, wins 85.4% — straight through the wall.

Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger played this game in the lab — Nobel Prize 2022. Nature carries no answer sheet.
